# Reading Biblical Hebrew & Greek
### A Practical Guide for English Speakers

The Bible was written in two ancient languages: **Hebrew** (Old Testament) and **Greek** (New Testament). This guide will help you sound out the original words as you study the interlinear text. You don't need to become fluent — even recognizing a few letters and roots will deepen your understanding of Scripture.

---

## Part 1: Biblical Hebrew

Hebrew is read **right to left**. It has 22 consonants and a system of dots and dashes (called "vowel points") added above and below the letters. The original text was written with consonants only — vowel points were added centuries later by Jewish scholars called the Masoretes.

### The Hebrew Alphabet (Aleph-Bet)

| Letter | Name | Sound | Example |
|--------|------|-------|---------|
| א | Aleph | silent (carries vowel) | **A**dam (אָדָם) |
| בּ / ב | Bet / Vet | **b** / **v** | **b**ayit (house) |
| גּ / ג | Gimel | **g** (hard, as in "go") | **g**adol (great) |
| דּ / ד | Dalet | **d** | **d**avar (word) |
| ה | He | **h** | **h**alal (praise) |
| ו | Vav | **v** (also used as vowel: o/u) | **v**e (and) |
| ז | Zayin | **z** | **z**emer (song) |
| ח | Chet | **ch** (guttural, like Scottish "lo**ch**") | **ch**esed (mercy) |
| ט | Tet | **t** | **t**ov (good) |
| י | Yod | **y** | **y**om (day) |
| כּ / כ / ך | Kaf / Khaf | **k** / **kh** (guttural) | **k**ohen (priest) |
| ל | Lamed | **l** | **l**ev (heart) |
| מ / ם | Mem | **m** | **m**ayim (water) |
| נ / ן | Nun | **n** | **n**avi (prophet) |
| ס | Samekh | **s** | **s**efer (book) |
| ע | Ayin | silent (guttural stop) | **ʿ**eved (servant) |
| פּ / פ / ף | Pe / Fe | **p** / **f** | **p**anim (face) |
| צ / ץ | Tsade | **ts** (as in "ca**ts**") | **ts**edek (righteousness) |
| ק | Qof | **k** (deep in throat) | **q**odesh (holy) |
| ר | Resh | **r** (slightly rolled) | **r**uach (spirit) |
| שׁ / שׂ | Shin / Sin | **sh** / **s** | **sh**alom (peace) |
| תּ / ת | Tav | **t** | **t**orah (instruction) |

**Five letters change shape at the end of a word:** כ→ך, מ→ם, נ→ן, פ→ף, צ→ץ. These are called "final forms" — same sound, different shape.

### Hebrew Vowel Points

Vowel points are small marks placed above or below consonants:

| Symbol | Name | Sound | Placed |
|--------|------|-------|--------|
| ָ  | Qamats | "ah" (f**a**ther) | below |
| ַ  | Patach | "ah" (shorter) | below |
| ֵ  | Tsere | "ay" (th**ey**) | below |
| ֶ  | Segol | "eh" (b**e**d) | below |
| ִ  | Chiriq | "ee" (mach**i**ne) | below |
| ֹ  | Cholem | "oh" (g**o**) | above-left |
| ֻ  | Qubbuts | "oo" (f**oo**d) | below |
| ְ  | Sheva | very brief "uh" or silent | below |

### Sounding Out a Hebrew Word

Let's try **בְּרֵאשִׁית** (bereshit — "in the beginning," Genesis 1:1):

Reading right to left:
1. **בְּ** = b + sheva = "be"
2. **רֵ** = r + tsere = "re"
3. **א** = aleph (silent, carries the vowel)
4. **שִׁ** = sh + chiriq = "shi"
5. **ית** = y + t = "it" (the chiriq under shin extends)

Together: **be-re-SHEET** → "In the beginning"

### Key Things to Know

- **Hebrew has no capital letters** — all letters are the same size
- **The dot in בּ vs ב** (called a dagesh) can change pronunciation: בּ = b, ב = v
- **God's name יהוה** (YHWH) appears nearly 7,000 times in the Old Testament. It is traditionally not pronounced, replaced with "Adonai" (Lord) when read aloud
- **Hebrew roots are usually 3 consonants.** The root מ-ל-כ (m-l-k) gives us מֶלֶךְ (melekh, "king"), מַלְכָּה (malkah, "queen"), and מַלְכוּת (malkhut, "kingdom"). Recognizing roots helps you see word families across Scripture

## Part 2: Biblical Greek (Koine)

The New Testament was written in **Koine Greek** — the common language of the ancient Mediterranean world. Unlike Hebrew, Greek reads **left to right** (like English) and uses vowels as full letters.

### The Greek Alphabet

| Letter | Name | Sound | Example |
|--------|------|-------|---------|
| Α α | Alpha | **a** (f**a**ther) | **a**gape (love) |
| Β β | Beta | **b** | **b**aptizo (baptize) |
| Γ γ | Gamma | **g** (hard, as in "go") | **g**rapho (write) |
| Δ δ | Delta | **d** | **d**oxa (glory) |
| Ε ε | Epsilon | **e** (short, as in "b**e**t") | **e**go (I) |
| Ζ ζ | Zeta | **z** or **dz** | **z**oe (life) |
| Η η | Eta | **ay** (as in "th**ey**") | **e**irene (peace) |
| Θ θ | Theta | **th** (as in "**th**ink") | **th**eos (God) |
| Ι ι | Iota | **ee** (as in "mach**i**ne") | **I**esous (Jesus) |
| Κ κ | Kappa | **k** | **k**yrios (Lord) |
| Λ λ | Lambda | **l** | **l**ogos (word) |
| Μ μ | Mu | **m** | **m**athetes (disciple) |
| Ν ν | Nu | **n** | **n**omos (law) |
| Ξ ξ | Xi | **x** (as in "bo**x**") | — |
| Ο ο | Omicron | **o** (short, as in "n**o**t") | **o**ikos (house) |
| Π π | Pi | **p** | **p**neuma (spirit) |
| Ρ ρ | Rho | **r** (slightly rolled) | — |
| Σ σ/ς | Sigma | **s** | **s**oter (savior) |
| Τ τ | Tau | **t** | — |
| Υ υ | Upsilon | **u** (French "**u**" or English "**oo**") | **hy**per (above) |
| Φ φ | Phi | **ph** / **f** | **ph**os (light) |
| Χ χ | Chi | **ch** (as in Scottish "lo**ch**") | **Ch**ristos (Christ) |
| Ψ ψ | Psi | **ps** (as in "li**ps**") | **ps**almos (psalm) |
| Ω ω | Omega | **oh** (long, as in "g**o**") | — |

**Note:** Sigma has two lowercase forms: **σ** in the middle of a word, **ς** at the end.

### Greek Breathing Marks

Greek vowels at the start of a word carry a breathing mark:
- **᾿** (smooth breathing) = no extra sound: ἐν = "en"
- **῾** (rough breathing) = adds an "h": ὁ = "ho"

This is why we write "Hebrews" with an H — the Greek Ἑβραῖος begins with rough breathing.

### Common Letter Combinations

| Combo | Sound | Example |
|-------|-------|---------|
| αι | "eye" or "ay" | αἷμα (haima, "blood") |
| ει | "ay" | εἰρήνη (eirene, "peace") |
| οι | "oy" | οἶκος (oikos, "house") |
| ου | "oo" | οὐρανός (ouranos, "heaven") |
| αυ | "ow" or "av" | αὐτός (autos, "he/self") |
| ευ | "ev" or "ef" | εὐαγγέλιον (euangelion, "gospel") |
| γγ | "ng" | ἄγγελος (angelos, "angel/messenger") |
| γκ | "nk" | — |
| γχ | "nch" | — |

### Sounding Out a Greek Word

Let's try **εὐαγγέλιον** (euangelion — "gospel" / "good news"):

1. **εὐ** = eu = "ev" (diphthong)
2. **αγ** = a + g = "an" (γ before γ = "ng")
3. **γέ** = ge = "ge"
4. **λι** = li = "li"
5. **ον** = on = "on"

Together: **ev-an-GE-li-on** → "good news" (from εὐ "good" + ἀγγελία "message")

### Greek Accents

Greek words carry accent marks (ά, ή, ώ) that show which syllable is stressed:
- **ἄνθρωπος** (AN-thro-pos) — "man/person" — stress on first syllable
- **θεός** (the-OS) — "God" — stress on last syllable

### Key Things to Know

- **Greek is an inflected language** — word endings change to show grammatical role. λόγος (logos) is "word" as subject; λόγον (logon) is "word" as object; λόγου (logou) is "of the word"
- **The word order is more flexible than English** because the endings carry the grammar. So "God loved the world" and "the world God loved" can mean the same thing in Greek
- **Many English words come from Greek:** theology (θεός + λόγος), philosophy (φιλέω + σοφία), angel (ἄγγελος), church (κυριακός), Bible (βιβλίον), psalm (ψαλμός), baptize (βαπτίζω), Christ (Χριστός), apostle (ἀπόστολος), evangel (εὐαγγέλιον)

## Tips for Using the Interlinear Text

1. **Start with familiar passages.** Try Genesis 1:1, Psalm 23:1, or John 3:16 — you already know what the text says, so you can focus on matching Hebrew/Greek words to their translations.

2. **Look for repeated words.** When you see the same Hebrew or Greek word appear multiple times, notice how it's consistently translated. This reveals patterns the author intended.

3. **Pay attention to word order.** Hebrew and Greek don't always follow English word order. The interlinear preserves the original order so you can see how the biblical author structured the sentence.

4. **Notice the root connections.** In Hebrew, שָׁלוֹם (shalom, "peace"), שָׁלֵם (shalem, "complete"), and יְרוּשָׁלַיִם (Yerushalayim, "Jerusalem") all share the root שׁ-ל-מ. These connections reveal layers of meaning.

5. **Don't worry about perfection.** Even scholars spend lifetimes studying these languages. Every word you recognize is a step closer to the original text. The goal is not mastery — it's encounter.
